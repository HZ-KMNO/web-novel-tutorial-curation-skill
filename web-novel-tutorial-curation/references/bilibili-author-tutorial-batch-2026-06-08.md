## Topic Classification

### Opening And Signing

Key sources: `1108016342-1-208.mp4_原文.docx`.

Transferable lessons:

- Early commercial review windows matter. The transcript emphasizes that the opening must show commercial value quickly, especially within the first few thousand words.
- The opening should make the protagonist, topic, conflict, current crisis, advantage, and reader question visible without wasting words.
- A title and synopsis should clearly reveal genre, selling point, protagonist situation, contradiction, and future direction.
- The first three chapters should stay focused on the protagonist; too many people too early dilute immersion.
- A crisis should force forward movement. If the protagonist can sit still without consequence, the opening loses pressure.

AI usage:

- When curating platform-facing tutorials, extract concrete timing windows, review points, and opening checks.
- Convert "write clearly" into operational fields: protagonist, old wound, current crisis, advantage, reader question, immediate next action.
- Do not preserve the tutorial's casual or vulgar phrasing; keep the mechanism.

### Built-In Traffic And System Satisfaction

Key sources: `1110161597-1-208.mp4_原文.docx`.

Transferable lessons:

- Some genres have built-in demand; innovation is not always safer than using a proven reader promise.
- System/golden-finger stories work when the reward loop is easy to understand and emotionally disproportionate: small effort, large visible reward.
- The protagonist's ordinary frustration creates entry-level immersion; the system answers a life pressure readers recognize.
- A system is strongest when it can escalate: reward amount, reward type, mission difficulty, status display, public recognition, and new consequence.

AI usage:

- Classify this as commercial genre mechanics, not literary craft.
- Preserve the principle "low-cost action -> high emotional reward -> visible escalation"; do not preserve crude examples.
- When summarizing, separate reader fantasy from ethical or platform-risk material.

### Multi-Point Burst Framework

Key sources: `470571321_u1-1-192.mp4_原文.docx`.

Transferable lessons:

- Start by fixing the opening hook and the target climax, then reverse-engineer the middle.
- A strong delayed hook must be repeatedly refreshed; readers should not forget the promised reveal.
- A large 100k-word frame can contain nested smaller frames. Example mechanism: first half builds accumulation and secondary expectation; second half shifts to payoff and broader conflict.
- Multiple lines should converge at the same burst point: main hook, emotional line, competition/event line, hidden enemy line, and public reveal line.
- Platform taste matters. A light, sweet, high-expectation platform should not receive needlessly bitter crisis design if that weakens completion rate.

AI usage:

- Use this source when curating advice about outline design, completion rate, 100k-word arcs, and platform-specific hook management.
- Capture the design method, not the specific "egg/wife" package.
- Extract a template: open hook, delayed payoff, midpoint reveal, repeated reminder, convergent lines, burst scene.

### Fan Fiction, Light Novel, Contrast, And Daily Comedy

Key sources: `1373692742-1-192.mp4_原文.docx`.

Transferable lessons:

- Fan fiction does not need to follow the original plot beat by beat; a fresh cut-in point and independent story can be more attractive.
- A strong one-line premise can carry large initial interest if it creates contrast and immediate imagination space.
- Daily-life scenes become dull when they only record routine. They need contrast, joke mechanics, relationship pressure, or expectation reversal.
- Character contrast is a reusable engine: scary face but gentle heart, villain label but good behavior, innocent action misread as dangerous.
- Golden fingers should be clear, visible, and immediately writable into scenes.

AI usage:

- Classify fan-fiction advice separately from original-fiction advice; mark copyright and derivative-work risks where needed.
- Convert contrast examples into general patterns rather than copying premises.
- Use this source for daily comedy, light romance, low-stakes scenes, and "not watery" everyday chapters.

### Story Boxes And Small-Goal Decomposition

Key sources: `1092606875-1-208.mp4_原文.docx`.

Transferable lessons:

- "No plot" often means the writer has not learned to break a desire into a series of executable goals.
- Web fiction can borrow game-flow logic: big goal -> many small goals -> obstacles -> rewards -> next goal.
- Reusable "story boxes" include apprenticeship, acquiring power, first encounter, joining a group, solving a local problem, obtaining a resource, exposing a secret, or escaping a threat.
- Reading should be purposeful. A writer should collect many variants of the same story box from different novels, then learn how to recombine them.
- Self-knowledge matters. A writer should use methods suited to current ability instead of pretending to write a once-in-a-century masterpiece immediately.

AI usage:

- Use this source when a user has an idea but no plot.
- Build a goal ladder before drafting: big objective, current objective, local obstacle, required resource, scene action, reward, new problem.
- Store story-box advice as a planning method, not as a copied scene library.
